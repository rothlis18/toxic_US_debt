The growing Muslim population in Western Europe challenges post-World War II secular-liberal integration models, which assumed rapid assimilation and declining religiosity. Demographic projections from Pew Research indicate divergent fertility and migration rates, with high religious observance persisting across second- and third-generation communities. Residential concentration and parallel legal and educational norms reshape municipal governance, influencing free-speech boundaries and welfare-state distribution. State-enforced secularism, exemplified by French laïcité, restricts religious expression, while multicultural accommodation fosters diversity but may dilute national identity. Political realignment toward nationalist-populist movements exploits ethnic tensions, advocating for stricter immigration controls and cultural homogeneity. Geographic self-sorting exacerbates these dynamics, with Muslims increasingly concentrated in urban areas. Non-Muslim European adaptation strategies involve navigating these trade-offs, balancing state control, cultural inclusivity, and electoral pressures.
