If foreign central banks, institutional investors, and primary dealers cease absorbing net new U.S. Treasury issuance, the immediate consequence is a sharp increase in Treasury yields due to reduced demand, leading to higher borrowing costs for the U.S. government. This shift disrupts the post-1971 petrodollar system, where U.S. fiscal deficits were funded at suppressed yields, as the global reserve-currency recycling mechanism breaks down. The compounding debt-service costs escalate, with negative real returns exacerbating the fiscal burden. Failed Treasury auctions would force the Federal Reserve into a dilemma: allow yields to spike, risking domestic banking insolvency and pension fund collapse, or implement yield-curve control and debt monetization, accelerating currency debasement and triggering stagflation. Quantitatively, the loss of the 'risk-free' Treasury benchmark would destabilize repo markets, leading to collateral liquidations and a liquidity crunch. Bilateral trade settlements would shift outside the SWIFT/dollar system, undermining U.S. financial hegemony. Domestically, severe austerity and entitlement restructuring would be necessary to manage the fiscal crisis, leading to economic contraction and social unrest. Globally, the financial unwind would trigger a reevaluation of reserve currencies, potentially leading to a multipolar currency system and increased volatility in international markets.
