The growing Muslim population in Western Europe is reshaping demographic, institutional, and sociopolitical dynamics. Post-World War II secular-liberal integration models assumed rapid assimilation and declining religiosity, but empirical data from Pew Research cohort modeling shows divergent fertility and migration rates, with high religious observance persisting across second- and third-generation communities. Residential concentration, parallel legal and educational norms, and electoral bloc politics are structurally reshaping municipal governance, free-speech boundaries, and welfare-state distribution. Non-Muslim European populations and state institutions are adapting through state-enforced secularism (e.g., French laïcité), multicultural accommodation, political realignment toward nationalist-populist movements, and geographic self-sorting. For instance, in France, the 2004 ban on headscarves in public schools reflects state-enforced secularism, while cities like Berlin and Amsterdam exhibit multicultural policies. Geographically, Muslims are self-sorting into predominantly Muslim neighborhoods, influencing local politics and social dynamics. These mechanisms create a complex interplay of integration and segregation, with significant trade-offs between secularism and cultural preservation, and between state control and individual freedoms.
