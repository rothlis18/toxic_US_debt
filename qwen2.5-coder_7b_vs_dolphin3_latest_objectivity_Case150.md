The growing Muslim population in Western Europe is reshaping the continent through demographic, institutional, and sociopolitical dynamics. Post-World War II secular-liberal integration models assumed rapid assimilation and declining religiosity, but empirical data from Pew Research cohort modeling and divergent fertility and migration rates indicate otherwise. Second- and third-generation Muslim communities exhibit high religious observance, challenging the assumption of assimilation.

Structural mechanics of adaptation include residential concentration, parallel legal and educational norms, and electoral bloc politics. These mechanisms reshape municipal governance, free-speech boundaries, and welfare-state distribution. For instance, the concentration of Muslim populations in certain neighborhoods leads to increased political representation and the formation of electoral blocs. Parallel legal norms, such as sharia courts, challenge state-enforced secularism, exemplified by France's laïcité. Educational norms, including the provision of religious education, further reinforce cultural and religious identity.

Non-Muslim European populations and state institutions adapt through a combination of state-enforced secularism, multicultural accommodation, political realignment toward nationalist-populist movements, and geographic self-sorting. State-enforced secularism, exemplified by France's laïcité, imposes strict limits on religious expression in public spaces. Multicultural accommodation, as seen in Germany, seeks to integrate diverse cultures through policies and programs. Political realignment toward nationalist-populist movements, such as the rise of the far right in Europe, reflects growing discontent with the status quo. Geographic self-sorting, where Muslims tend to cluster in certain areas, exacerbates social and political tensions.

In conclusion, the growing Muslim population in Western Europe is driving significant structural changes through demographic concentration, parallel legal and educational norms, and electoral bloc politics. These dynamics challenge the post-World War II secular-liberal integration model, necessitating a reevaluation of state policies and institutional frameworks.
