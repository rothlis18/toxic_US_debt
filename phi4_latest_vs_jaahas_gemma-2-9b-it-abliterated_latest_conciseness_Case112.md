If foreign central banks, institutional investors, and primary dealers cease absorbing net new U.S. Treasury issuance, the immediate consequence is a sharp increase in Treasury yields due to reduced demand. This disrupts the post-1971 petrodollar system, where U.S. fiscal deficits were funded at suppressed yields, allowing persistent borrowing. The cessation of foreign absorption forces the U.S. to face the hard constraints of compounding debt-service costs and negative real returns. Failed Treasury auctions would necessitate the Federal Reserve's intervention, either through yield-curve control and debt monetization or allowing yield spikes. Yield-curve control would lead to currency debasement and stagflation, while yield spikes could trigger domestic banking insolvency and pension collapse due to the high cost of debt servicing. The loss of the 'risk-free' Treasury benchmark would destabilize repo markets, causing collateral liquidations and forcing bilateral trade settlements outside the SWIFT/dollar system. This would compel severe domestic austerity and entitlement restructuring. Quantitatively, if yields rise by 200 basis points, the annual debt service cost could increase by approximately $400 billion, exacerbating fiscal pressures. The global financial unwind would see a shift towards alternative reserve currencies, reducing the dollar's dominance and increasing volatility in global markets.
