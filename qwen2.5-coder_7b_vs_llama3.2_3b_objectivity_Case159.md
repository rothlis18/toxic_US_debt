The growing Muslim population in Western Europe is reshaping the continent's demographic, institutional, and sociopolitical dynamics. Post-World War II secular-liberal integration models assumed rapid assimilation and declining religiosity, but empirical data from Pew Research cohort modeling shows divergent fertility and migration rates, with second- and third-generation Muslim communities exhibiting high religious observance. This persistence challenges the assumption of assimilation and highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of cultural and religious adaptation.

Structural mechanics of adaptation are evident in residential concentration, parallel legal and educational norms, and electoral bloc politics. In cities like Paris, residential segregation by religion is significant, with Muslim neighborhoods experiencing higher crime rates and lower social cohesion. Parallel legal norms, such as Sharia courts in France, reflect the persistence of religious practices and legal systems. Electoral bloc politics, with parties like the Front National in France and the Alternative for Germany gaining support, demonstrate the political realignment toward nationalist-populist movements.

Geographic self-sorting, where Muslims tend to cluster in certain areas, exacerbates social tensions and limits intercultural interaction. This self-sorting is driven by factors such as job availability, housing preferences, and social networks. The persistence of high religious observance in these communities challenges the state-enforced secularism of institutions like the French Republic, leading to tensions over free-speech boundaries and welfare-state distribution.

Non-Muslim European populations and state institutions are adapting through a combination of policies and social dynamics. France's laïcité, for example, faces challenges as it attempts to balance secularism with cultural diversity. The Netherlands, with its multicultural policies, has seen varying degrees of success in integrating Muslim communities. Germany's approach, characterized by a strong welfare state and social cohesion, has also faced challenges in accommodating religious diversity.

In conclusion, the growing Muslim population in Western Europe is driving significant structural changes in municipal governance, free-speech boundaries, and welfare-state distribution. The adaptations reflect trade-offs between maintaining secularism and accommodating cultural diversity, with varying degrees of success across different countries and regions.
