The growing Muslim population in Western Europe is reshaping the continent's demographic, institutional, and sociopolitical dynamics. Post-World War II secular-liberal integration models, which assumed rapid assimilation and declining religiosity, are increasingly challenged by empirical demographic projections and divergent fertility and migration rates. Second- and third-generation Muslim communities exhibit high religious observance, defying predictions of assimilation.

Structural mechanics of adaptation are evident in residential concentration, parallel legal and educational norms, and electoral bloc politics. Residential concentration, driven by factors like housing affordability and discriminatory practices, exacerbates social tensions and limits integration. Parallel legal and educational norms, such as sharia courts and religious education, undermine state-enforced secularism. Electoral bloc politics, shaped by economic interests and social inequalities, favor nationalist-populist movements.

To quantify trade-offs, state-enforced secularism in France, exemplified by laïcité, faces challenges in maintaining social cohesion and political stability. Multicultural accommodation, while promoting diversity, risks diluting national identity and economic efficiency. Nationalist-populist movements, fueled by economic grievances and social anxieties, promise strong governance but may undermine democratic institutions. Geographic self-sorting, influenced by housing affordability and resource distribution, perpetuates segregation and limits integration.

Connecting to broader systems, the dynamics of Muslim population growth are intertwined with global capitalism, migration patterns, and resource distribution. Economic disparities and resource scarcity drive migration, exacerbating social tensions and political instability. To achieve long-term success, Western European states must navigate these complex dynamics through a combination of policies that promote economic growth, social cohesion, and political stability.
