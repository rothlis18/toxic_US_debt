The growing Muslim population in Western Europe challenges the post-World War II secular-liberal integration model, which assumed rapid assimilation and declining religiosity. According to Pew Research cohort modeling, Muslim populations are projected to increase from 5% in 2010 to 10% by 2050, driven by higher fertility rates (2.1 vs. 1.6 for non-Muslims) and continued migration. Residential concentration of Muslims in urban areas exacerbates social cohesion issues, leading to parallel legal and educational norms that reinforce cultural differences. Electoral bloc politics, particularly in France and the Netherlands, have seen significant gains for nationalist-populist parties, reflecting growing discontent with multicultural policies. State-enforced secularism, exemplified by French laïcité, restricts religious expression in public spaces, while multicultural accommodation policies, such as Germany's, provide legal protections for religious practices but may dilute national identity. Geographic self-sorting, where Muslims cluster in certain neighborhoods, further entrenches cultural divides. Quantitatively, areas with high Muslim concentration experience higher crime rates and lower social trust. Economically, Muslim communities face higher unemployment rates, particularly among women and youth, despite similar educational attainment levels. A simplified model reveals feedback loops where demographic changes drive institutional responses, which in turn shape sociopolitical outcomes. The most effective adaptation strategy appears to be a balanced approach that combines state-enforced secularism with targeted integration programs, such as language training and vocational education, to enhance economic mobility and social integration. This strategy minimizes the risk of nationalist-populist movements while preserving national identity and cohesion.
