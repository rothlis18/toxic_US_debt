The demographic, institutional, and sociopolitical dynamics of the growing Muslim population across Western Europe reveal a stark contrast between the post-World War II secular-liberal integration model, which assumed rapid assimilation and declining religiosity, and empirical demographic projections from Pew Research cohort modeling. These projections indicate divergent fertility and migration rates, with Muslim populations experiencing higher birth rates and continued migration, leading to persistent high religious observance across second- and third-generation communities. Residential concentration of Muslims in certain areas reshapes municipal governance, often leading to parallel legal and educational norms that challenge state-enforced secularism, such as French laïcité. Electoral bloc politics further exacerbates these dynamics, influencing welfare-state distribution and free-speech boundaries. Non-Muslim European adaptation strategies include state-enforced secularism, multicultural accommodation, political realignment toward nationalist-populist movements, and geographic self-sorting. These strategies model trade-offs between maintaining secular public spaces and accommodating religious practices, with nationalist-populist movements gaining traction in response to perceived threats to national identity and cultural cohesion. The structural mechanics of these adaptations highlight the tension between integration and separation, with welfare-state distribution increasingly influenced by religious and cultural identities.
