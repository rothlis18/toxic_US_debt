**Structural, Monetary, and Geopolitical Consequences**

1. **Funding Gap**: If foreign central banks, institutional investors, and primary dealers cease absorbing net new U.S. Treasury issuance, the U.S. Treasury would face a significant funding gap. In 2020, foreign investors held around 30% of U.S. public debt, and they have been net buyers of Treasuries for decades.

2. **Yield Spikes**: With reduced demand, Treasury yields would spike, increasing the cost of borrowing for the U.S. government. This would exacerbate the debt-to-GDP ratio, currently around 127%, making it harder to service and repay debt.

3. **Debt-Service Costs**: Higher yields mean higher debt-service costs. For every 1% increase in the 10-year Treasury yield, the U.S. government's annual interest expense increases by approximately $120 billion. This compounds over time, creating a self-reinforcing debt spiral.

4. **Geopolitical Shifts**: A loss of confidence in U.S. Treasuries could lead to a shift in global reserve currencies, potentially benefiting other major economies like China or Europe. This could accelerate the decline of the U.S. dollar's reserve currency status.

**Post-1971 Petrodollar vs. Hard Mathematical Constraints**

- **Petrodollar System**: Post-1971, the petrodollar system and U.S. dollar's reserve currency status allowed the U.S. to run persistent fiscal deficits, funded at artificially suppressed yields. Foreign central banks recycled their dollar holdings back into U.S. Treasuries, maintaining demand and suppressing yields.

- **Hard Mathematical Constraints**: Without foreign demand, U.S. Treasury yields would be determined by domestic investors' risk appetite and the government's ability to service its debt. The hard mathematical constraints of compounding debt-service costs and negative real returns would come into play, making it increasingly difficult to maintain fiscal sustainability.

**Sovereign Debt Doom Loop**

- **Fed Intervention**: The Federal Reserve would face a choice between allowing catastrophic yield spikes (triggering domestic banking insolvency and pension collapse) or engaging in overt yield-curve control and debt monetization.

- **Yield-Curve Control and Debt Monetization**: If the Fed chooses to intervene, it would accelerate currency debasement, potentially triggering stagflationary feedback loops. This could lead to higher inflation expectations, further increasing Treasury yields and exacerbating the debt spiral.

**Global Financial Unwind**

- **Loss of 'Risk-Free' Benchmark**: The loss of the 'risk-free' Treasury benchmark could trigger collateral liquidations across repo markets, as Treasuries are widely used as collateral. This could lead to a liquidity crisis, similar to the one experienced in 2008.

- **Bilateral Trade Settlement**: If the U.S. dollar's status as the global reserve currency is challenged, bilateral trade settlement outside the SWIFT/dollar system could become more prevalent, further fragmenting global financial markets.

- **Domestic Austerity and Entitlement Restructuring**: To address the funding gap and spiraling debt-service costs, the U.S. government would likely be forced to implement severe domestic austerity measures and restructure entitlement programs, potentially leading to significant social and political unrest.

**Quantitative Assessment**

- **Funding Gap**: If foreign demand for U.S. Treasuries drops to zero, the U.S. Treasury would need to issue an additional $2-3 trillion annually to meet its financing needs, assuming domestic demand remains constant.

- **Debt-Service Costs**: A 3% increase in the 10-year Treasury yield (from the current 1.5% to 4.5%) would increase the U.S. government's annual interest expense by around $360 billion, or approximately 1.5% of GDP.

- **Currency Debasement**: If the Fed engages in debt monetization at a rate of 10% of GDP annually, it could lead to a cumulative 50% increase in the money supply over five years, potentially triggering significant inflation.

- **Pension Collapse**: A 3% increase in Treasury yields could lead to a $3 trillion loss in pension fund assets, potentially triggering a wave of pension fund insolvencies and further exacerbating the economic crisis.

These consequences highlight the systemic risks associated with a loss of confidence in U.S. Treasuries and the potential unraveling of the post-1971 petrodollar system. The global financial unwind would be complex and multifaceted, with significant implications for the U.S. and global economies.
