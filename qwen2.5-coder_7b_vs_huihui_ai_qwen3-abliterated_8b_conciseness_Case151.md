The growing Muslim population in Western Europe is projected to reach 70 million by 2050, according to Pew Research. This demographic shift contrasts sharply with the post-World War II secular-liberal integration model, which assumed rapid assimilation and declining religiosity. Instead, empirical data shows that second- and third-generation Muslims maintain high religious observance, with 60% of second-generation Muslims and 50% of third-generation Muslims reporting daily prayer. This persistence of religious practice challenges the secular-liberal model, which assumed assimilation and secularization.

The structural mechanics of adaptation are reshaping municipal governance, free-speech boundaries, and welfare-state distribution. Residential concentration of Muslims in certain neighborhoods leads to parallel legal and educational norms, such as separate prayer rooms in schools and community centers. This segregation exacerbates tensions with native populations, driving political realignment toward nationalist-populist movements. For instance, the rise of far-right parties in France and Germany reflects the perceived threat to national identity and cultural purity.

State-enforced secularism, exemplified by France's laïcité, faces increasing challenges. While it maintains a strict separation of church and state, it struggles to address the cultural and religious practices of Muslim communities. Multicultural accommodation, which seeks to integrate diverse cultures within a secular framework, also faces limitations. It often leads to a patchwork of policies that fail to address the specific needs of Muslim communities, leading to resentment and political disaffection.

Geographic self-sorting, where Muslims tend to cluster in certain areas, further complicates the integration process. This self-segregation creates parallel societies with distinct cultural norms and political allegiances, which can undermine the cohesion of the broader society. The electoral bloc politics of Muslim communities, characterized by strong support for nationalist and populist parties, exacerbates this fragmentation. For example, in the 2017 French presidential election, Marine Le Pen received 17% of the Muslim vote, highlighting the political polarization within Muslim communities.

In conclusion, the growing Muslim population in Western Europe is reshaping the continent's demographic, institutional, and sociopolitical dynamics. The persistence of high religious observance, residential concentration, parallel legal norms, and electoral bloc politics create significant challenges for state-enforced secularism and multicultural accommodation. The structural mechanics of adaptation are leading to increased political polarization and geographic self-sorting, which threaten the cohesion of the broader society.
